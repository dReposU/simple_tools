# Adapting to left-hand-only PC use

**I’d start with voice input, a few Windows settings, and moving your mouse to the left.** You can try these before buying equipment.

## Work context

**What do you mainly do on your PC—programming, writing, spreadsheets, design, or something else—and is your left hand your dominant hand?**

You use the PC for programming, writing, and Google Sheets. Your left hand is your nondominant hand. You also need a silent way to write long texts during meetings or whenever speaking is not possible.

## Methods without buying anything

### Silent typing and long-form writing

**For silent, long-form writing, I’d build a one-handed typing setup and use prediction and shortcuts to reduce the keystrokes.** Since your left hand is your nondominant hand, allow yourself a slower pace while you learn.

#### Your existing physical keyboard

**Start with your existing keyboard and Sticky Keys.** Move the keyboard so the keys you’re using are comfortably in front of your left hand. Practice with short, useful text—emails or code comments. Aim for accuracy and comfortable movement before speed.

**Turn on Sticky Keys.** Go to **Settings → Accessibility → Keyboard → Sticky keys**. This lets you press shortcuts sequentially—for example, press and release **Ctrl**, then **S** to save. [Keyboard accessibility settings](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/accessibility/windows/make-your-mouse-keyboard-and-other-input-devices-easier-to-use)

#### Word prediction

**Try word prediction for writing.** In Windows 11, go to **Settings → Time & language → Typing → Show text suggestions when typing on the physical keyboard**. This offers word completions while you type. Try it in your usual writing apps; keep it only if selecting suggestions saves you effort. [Microsoft instructions](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/accessibility/windows/enable-text-suggestions-in-windows)

#### Snippets and repeated text

**Use snippets for programming and repeated text.** Create short triggers for recurring paragraphs, code structures, and formulas. For example, `xrec` could insert a standard status update that you then edit.

### Programming and keyboard shortcuts

#### Snippets in VS Code

In VS Code, snippets can include placeholders you move through with **Tab**; enabling `"editor.tabCompletion": "on"` lets you insert snippets by typing their prefix and pressing Tab. [VS Code snippets](https://code.visualstudio.com/docs/editing/userdefinedsnippets)

#### Key and shortcut remapping

**Bring difficult keys closer.** I’d consider remapping **Caps Lock to Backspace**, then giving frequently used editor commands comfortable left-hand shortcuts. PowerToys Keyboard Manager supports key and shortcut remapping. For programming, prioritize easy access to **Enter, Backspace, arrows, brackets, and quotation marks**. [PowerToys documentation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/powertoys/keyboard-manager)

### Google Sheets

Start with **Tab/Shift+Tab** and **Enter/Shift+Enter** to move between cells. **Alt + /** opens the tool finder, letting you search for commands without navigating menus. Sticky Keys helps with the combinations. [Sheets shortcuts](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/181110)

### Voice input when speaking is possible

#### Dictation for longer writing

**Use your voice for longer writing.** Click a text field and press **Windows + H** to dictate emails, documents, and messages. It needs a microphone and internet connection. Your existing headset is enough to try it. [Microsoft’s instructions](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/accessibility/windows/use-voice-typing-to-talk-instead-of-type-on-your-pc)

#### Voice Access for controlling the computer

**Try Voice Access for controlling the computer.** On Windows 11, go to **Settings → Accessibility → Speech → Voice access**. You can open apps, navigate, click, and dictate. After the initial language download, it works offline. I’d make this your first experiment if you spend hours on the PC. [Setup guide](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/accessibility/windows/voice-access/set-up-voice-access)

### Mouse setup, positioning, and breaks

**Make the mouse comfortable for your left hand.** Move it close to the left side of your keyboard. Under **Settings → Bluetooth & devices → Mouse**, try setting the primary button to **Right**, so your left index finger performs the main click. Also try **ClickLock** under **Additional mouse settings → Buttons**, which makes dragging possible without continuously holding the button. [Mouse settings](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/hardware/input-devices/change-mouse-settings)

**Reduce reaching and repeated work.** Keep your wrist straight and elbow near your body; reposition the keyboard as needed. Use saved templates for repeated messages and take frequent short breaks. A compact keyboard may help keep everything within reach. [Workstation guidance](https://www.osha.gov/etools/computer-workstations/components/keyboards/)

## Physical keyboards and optional equipment

### Mirrored keyboards

**If you’ll need this for months, investigate a mirrored keyboard.** These let your left hand type both halves of the alphabet from the left side. For example, the Matias Half Keyboard uses the spacebar as a modifier to access the other half. This requires practice; before buying, check how comfortably you can enter numbers, programming symbols, and shortcuts. [How it works](https://matias.ca/halfkeyboard/)

## Professional support

If this will last a while, ask your rehabilitation team about an **occupational therapist who can assess your computer setup**. I’d trial specialized one-handed keyboards before committing to one.

## Suggested starting setup

**My starting choice for you:** familiar keyboard, Sticky Keys, snippets, and a few remapped keys. Consider mirroring if reaching across the keyboard remains the main obstacle.
